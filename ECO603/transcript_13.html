13. Time series: corrections, heteroskedasticity and responses

Choose a time-series uncertainty method and estimate a response at a named horizon.

Core lesson · Appendix

Core lesson

01. Choose between revising the mean, estimator and covariance

The residual diagnostics have shown why an ordered sample needs more than an independent-observation uncertainty formula. We now compare three responses: add justified dynamics to the conditional mean, specify and estimate an error process, or keep the coefficient calculation and estimate a dependence-aware covariance.

The continuing monthly traffic application and the constructed quarterly response example make these choices concrete. Each method needs exogeneity and approximation conditions appropriate to its task. A corrected standard error does not repair an omitted cause, and a fitted error process does not establish a policy experiment.

Revise the conditional mean when the economic model needs additional dynamics.
Compare model-based error transformations with HAC covariance under their stated assumptions.

02. Use HAC to account for unequal spread and time dependence

Newey–West keeps the OLS coefficients and changes their estimated uncertainty.

Recall how a district slope uses the part of its regressor left after adjusting for controls. For a monthly slope, call that remainder r X at date t. Let a at t be r X times the fitted error at that date. These products record the contribution of each date to uncertainty about the slope.

In a sample with dependent months, we add cross-date products as well as the same-date squares. Newey–West adds pairs up to a chosen lag and gives smaller weights to more distant pairs. At lag four, the weights for gaps one through four are 0.8, 0.6, 0.4 and 0.2. Divide by the square of the total squared regressor remainders to put the result in slope-variance units. The displayed expression omits a finite-sample scaling factor.

H A C stands for heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent. A justified approximation needs a suitable time-series model and dependence conditions. The lag choice should reflect the frequency, expected dependence and available sample. The fitted regression and its coefficient stay fixed when we change only the covariance calculation.

\(a_t=rX_t\widehat u_t\), where \(rX_t\) is the regressor remainder after the other inputs.
\(w_h=1-h/(L+1)\) for \(h=1,\ldots,L\). For \(L=4\): \(0.8,0.6,0.4,0.2\).
Variance estimate: \(\dfrac{\sum_t a_t^2+2\sum_{h=1}^L w_h\sum_{t=h+1}^T a_ta_{t-h}}{(\sum_t rX_t^2)^2}\).

03. Predictable error variance is different from a predictable error mean

ARCH describes volatility clustering, not necessarily directional error predictability.

Autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity, abbreviated ARCH, allows the conditional variance to depend on past squared errors. In an ARCH one model, the error can still have zero conditional mean while its conditional variance is alpha zero plus alpha one times the previous squared error. Positive alpha one makes large absolute shocks tend to follow large absolute shocks.

A conventional ARCH LM diagnostic regresses squared fitted residuals on a constant and selected lags of squared residuals. For the one-lag version, the null is alpha one equal to zero: the previous squared error has no role in this variance equation. Its reference distribution needs the model and approximation conditions. The actual monthly traffic example reports a lag-one statistic of about 0.350 and p-value 0.554. This is a failure to reject that specified diagnostic null, not proof that all forms of changing variance are absent. Neither result settles exogeneity.

\(E[u_t\mid I_{t-1}]=0\), \(E[u_t^2\mid I_{t-1}]=\alpha_0+\alpha_1u_{t-1}^2\), \(\alpha_0>0,\alpha_1\ge0\).
Conventional diagnostic: regress residual squares on a constant and their selected lags.
Actual monthly diagnostic: \(\chi_1^2\approx0.350\), \(p\approx0.554\).

07. Convert the fitted log difference into percent

Use 100[exp(b)−1] for the exact contrast between exponentiated fitted values.

From the log-model lesson, an indicator coefficient b gives a percentage contrast in exponentiated fitted outcomes of 100 times exp of b minus one. At b about 0.09545, this is about 10.02 percent. The coefficient is uncertain, so the transformed contrast is uncertain too.

Near this coefficient, the transformation's local slope is 100 times exp of b, about 110.02. A small coefficient change of 0.01 therefore changes the contrast by about 1.10 percentage units. Multiplying this local slope by the HAC standard error, about 0.01890, gives a transformed standard error near 2.08 percentage units. This local propagation of uncertainty is the delta method. Stata's nlcom performs it using the fitted covariance. A z statistic divides the transformed estimate by its approximate standard error and uses a normal reference. As before, a contrast in arithmetic mean accidents requires the relevant retransformation factors to match or be modeled.

09. Transform a serial-error equation step by step

Subtracting the error carry-over also transforms the regressors.

Suppose the outcome equation is Y at t equal to an intercept a plus beta times X at t plus u at t. Model the error as rho times the previous error plus a new mean-zero innovation. For a later observation, subtract rho times the entire previous outcome equation from the current equation. The new error is u at t minus rho times the previous u, which equals the innovation.

The left side becomes current Y minus rho times previous Y. The regressor becomes current X minus rho times previous X, and the intercept becomes a times one minus rho. Transforming only the outcome would leave a different model. If the error process and exogeneity assumptions are appropriate, this transformed equation removes that particular error carry-over and can use information more efficiently.

\(Y_t=a+\beta X_t+u_t,\quad u_t=\rho u_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\)
\(Y_t-\rho Y_{t-1}=a(1-\rho)+\beta(X_t-\rho X_{t-1})+\varepsilon_t\)

10. Make the transformation feasible

Prais–Winsten estimates the carry-over and retains the first observation.

The carry-over rho is usually unknown. Prais–Winsten estimates it from the fitted error pattern and uses that estimate to transform the outcome and every regressor. With rho equal to 0.5, an outcome pair of ten followed by twelve gives a transformed later outcome of twelve minus five, or seven. A regressor pair of two followed by three becomes three minus one, or two. The intercept column becomes one half.

For stationary AR(1) errors with absolute rho below one, the first observation has a different variance from the transformed later innovations. Prais–Winsten retains it with a square-root-of-one-minus-rho-squared weight. The method therefore uses a structured error model and can change coefficients as well as standard errors. Its potential precision gain depends on that structure and the exogeneity assumptions being credible; it is not interchangeable with retaining OLS and using HAC.

For \(\rho=0.5\): \(12-0.5(10)=7\), \(3-0.5(2)=2\)
First-observation weight: \(\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\)
Estimate rho, transform all columns, and fit the transformed equation.

11. Prais–Winsten models an AR(1) error process

Its additional structure can change the fit.

Prais–Winsten estimates an AR(1) carry-over in the errors and transforms the regression accordingly, including a special first-observation adjustment. In the original accident equation, its law coefficient is 0.101 with SE 0.020. It is not required to equal the OLS coefficient.

The potential efficiency benefit depends on the error model and appropriate regressor assumptions. If that structure is not credible, the resulting precision claim may be misleading. Compare this with HAC, which addresses selected covariance contributions without imposing exactly this AR(1) transformation. A method is useful when its assumptions fit the problem, not because its name contains correction.

12. Specify the known quarterly data-generating rule

A stable process gives a transparent response benchmark.

Change applications explicitly: we now use a constructed quarterly inflation experiment, not the historical accident data. Pi denotes inflation. Its rule is 0.6 times last quarter's inflation plus 0.8 times the current shock s plus a separate innovation epsilon. The shock and innovation draws are independent across dates and of each other in this experiment.

Define the intervention as increasing the current s by one while holding all other external draws and the previous inflation value fixed. The inflation mechanism is then allowed to carry the change forward. Inflation rises by 0.8 immediately, 0.48 next quarter and 0.288 the quarter after. At horizon h, measured in quarters after the initial date, the known response is 0.8 times 0.6 to power h. This response is known because we specified the causal mechanism and its inputs; it is not a fitted historical policy effect.

13. A local projection asks about a specified future outcome

Define the shock and horizon before the coefficient.

A local projection estimates a separate regression for each future horizon. At horizon zero, regress current inflation on the current shock and the chosen earlier information. At horizon four, use inflation four quarters later as the outcome but keep the shock dated at the initial quarter. The shock coefficient then has inflation-units per unit of that initial shock.

In the displayed equation, h is the horizon, a subscript h is its intercept, b subscript h its shock coefficient and c subscript h the coefficient on earlier inflation. The error is also horizon specific: different future outcomes leave different unexplained components. Because the constructed shock is independent of the other innovations, this experiment lets us compare estimates with the known intervention response. In observed data, the shock and control choices require their own identification argument.

\(\pi_{t+h}=a_h+b_hs_t+c_h\pi_{t-1}+u^{(h)}_{t+h}\)
\(h\): quarters ahead; \(b_h\): response per current shock unit.

14. The horizon coefficient has outcome-per-shock units

Do not confuse a future value with a cumulative sum.

At horizon four, the coefficient concerns inflation four quarters after the shock. It is not the sum of responses over the first four quarters. A cumulative response requires a separately defined sum, with uncertainty reflecting covariance among the estimated responses.

Write the economic sentence before calculating: a one-unit shock is associated with a fitted difference in the outcome at a specified future date, conditional on the included information. In the controlled simulation, the shock's independence supports the intended causal response. In a real application, that causal sentence needs a justified shock measure and appropriate controls.

15. Inspect the estimated path without forcing it to match the truth

Finite-sample response estimates can be noisy.

The plotted points and SEs come from the Stata regressions at horizons zero through eight. The line is the known simulation response. Estimates need not equal the line in any particular generated dataset, and an individual 95% interval need not contain its true value.

This is not a reason to select a different random seed after seeing an inconvenient result. The simulation helps us learn that sampling uncertainty remains even with a correctly identified constructed shock. At longer horizons, fewer usable observations and greater unexplained variation can make the path less precise. Report that uncertainty instead of drawing a smooth curve that hides the actual estimates.

17. Future-outcome errors can overlap across dates

A sensible mean model can still leave serial dependence.

At a given horizon, two adjacent starting dates can use outcomes affected by some of the same future innovations. To see overlap simply, consider a two-period outcome sum whose unexplained component at starting date t is v at t plus one plus v at t plus two. The next starting date's error includes v at t plus two plus v at t plus three. Both contain the same middle innovation.

This is a constructed overlap illustration, not a claim that the inflation experiment has exactly that two-term error formula. It explains why independent underlying innovations can still produce related regression errors at a future horizon. The relevant dependence belongs to the error process for that horizon, so the covariance choice should reflect its timing and other maintained conditions. Overlap does not make every estimated response biased, but it matters for inference.

18. Pointwise intervals do not cover an entire response path at the same rate

Multiple horizons change the inferential question.

A pointwise 95-percent interval is designed for one specified horizon considered by itself. Covering every point on a response path is a different event: every horizon's interval must succeed together. For illustration, if nine separate coverage events were independent and each had probability 0.95, their joint probability would be 0.95 to the ninth power, about 0.63.

Actual local-projection estimates across horizons are correlated, so 0.63 is not the coverage probability of this graph. The example shows why individual confidence levels do not automatically provide the same simultaneous confidence level. A claim about the whole path, a peak or a cumulative response needs an inference procedure for that joint quantity, using its covariance and an appropriate reference argument.

Pointwise coverage: one stated horizon.
Simultaneous coverage: the complete stated collection of horizons.
Illustration only, with independent events: \(0.95^9\approx0.63\).

19. Controlling a mechanism can change the response being studied

A current mediator is not just another lag control.

Consider a new causal story for observed inflation: a shock changes expectations, and expectations help determine future inflation. This expectations variable is not part of the small simulation's specified equation. We add it here to analyze a possible control choice. It is a mediator when it carries part of the shock's total effect.

Other news may also affect expectations and future inflation. Select expectations in the graph. Doing so blocks part of the shock's mechanism and can open a path through other news because expectations are a collider on that alternative route. A smaller adjusted shock coefficient would therefore not prove that its difference is a mediated effect. The intervention target, timing, support and mediator–outcome assumptions must be stated before interpreting the adjusted comparison.

20. Distinguish an estimated shock from a known innovation

Measurement and first-step uncertainty can matter.

In the simulation, the independent shock is observed exactly because we generated it. In applied macroeconomics, a shock may instead be inferred from a first regression, a statistical decomposition or a separate source of economic variation. An inferred shock contains estimation uncertainty and may mix the intended innovation with other economic news.

Putting that estimated series into a response regression is a two-step procedure. Inference must consider how the first-step estimation affects the response estimate, including dependence between the steps. A suitable joint method or a resampling procedure that repeats both estimation steps on appropriately redrawn histories depends on the actual construction and target. Before choosing one, explain what intervention the shock is meant to represent and why its variation is independent of omitted future-outcome causes. We develop the outside-instrument argument in Meeting 14.

Known simulated shock: observed by construction.
Estimated empirical shock: identification and first-step uncertainty need separate arguments.

25. HAC estimates covariance of regressor-error scores across dates

Autocorrelation of errors and covariance of the coefficient estimates are connected by the regressors.

Technical appendix

For a time-series regression, define the regressor-error product vector s t as the regressor vector x t multiplied by the model error u t. The long-run covariance sums covariance of these vectors across all date separations, when that sum and the required limit approximation are well defined. With Q equal to the limiting regressor second moment, the coefficient covariance is approximately Q inverse times this long-run covariance times Q inverse, divided by T.

A Newey–West estimate uses fitted residuals, a finite lag window and weights that taper with lag distance. Here bandwidth means the number of lagged cross-products kept in that window. Its consistency requires suitable exogeneity, moments, dependence and bandwidth conditions. Changing the lag choice changes which empirical cross-date products contribute. It does not remove an omitted cause or automatically guarantee accurate inference in a short persistent sample.

\(s_t=x_tu_t\), \(S=\sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty}E[s_ts_{t-h}\prime]\), when the sum and limits are justified.
Approximate coefficient covariance: \(Q^{-1}SQ^{-1}/T\).
Newey–West estimates a weighted, truncated version using fitted residuals.

26. Interpret the lag in calendar time

Interpret the selected maximum lag using months or quarters.

Further study

Our maximum lag L must be interpreted using the data frequency, meaning how often we observe the series. These observations are monthly, so lag(4) allows covariance contributions through four months. In quarterly data, one observation covers three months; the same lag option would reach four quarters, or one year.

This maximum lag is also called a bandwidth in this setting. It controls how far apart two observations can be and still contribute directly to the estimated covariance. It does not add lagged accidents, lagged policy variables or more seasonal controls to the regression equation. Adding those variables would change the mean model; selecting this window changes the uncertainty calculation.

Move L in the graph and watch which weights remain positive. A larger window includes more estimated covariance contributions, but it does not guarantee a larger or better SE. 35. The first observation needs its own GLS transformation

Quasi-differencing and ordinary first differences impose different error models.

Technical appendix

For an AR one error model with absolute rho below one, subtract rho times the lagged regression equation at dates after the first. This gives the innovation e t as the transformed error. If the initial error is drawn from the stationary AR one distribution, multiplying the first row of the entire regression by square root of one minus rho squared puts its error on the same variance scale.

Prais–Winsten keeps this adjusted first row; Cochrane–Orcutt drops it. In practice rho is estimated, so feasible GLS needs a credible error specification and additional conditions for its large-sample claims. Transform the intercept and every regressor as well as the outcome. Ordinary first differencing instead uses rho equal to one in the algebra; it is not interchangeable with a stationary-error GLS model.

For \(t\ge2\): \(Y_t-\rho Y_{t-1}=(1-\rho)\beta_0+\beta_1(X_t-\rho X_{t-1})+e_t\).
Stationary first row: \((Y_1^\star,X_1^\star,1^\star)=\sqrt{1-\rho^2}(Y_1,X_1,1)\).
